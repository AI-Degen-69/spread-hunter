Where the rescue money went.
We built the detective tool — and it caught something we didn't expect.

When only one side of a two-sided trade fills, the engine sells the leftover piece to stop the bleeding. Those emergency sales had been quietly eating the profits. This change builds a read-only detective that reopens last month's rehearsal and lists every one of those sales, worst first — and shows which ones were a late decision, which were a market that had already vanished, and which were never a decision at all.

The three questions we asked the data

Before this tool existed, nobody could answer these without redoing the whole thing by hand. Now it is one command, and the honest answer is sometimes "the data can't tell us."

Would waiting longer have saved it?Q1 · the missing book
Can't be answered

To know whether the other side could still have been caught, we would need the full price book from that exact minute. We only kept price samples, and none of them cover this rehearsal. Rather than guess, the tool says so out loud. An earlier rehearsal did answer it: of 13 pieces that later found a partner, the ones still waiting after 5 to 120 seconds rescued nothing extra.

Did we act too late, or was the bid already gone?Q2 · why each sale fired
Mostly unknown — now fixable

The rehearsal predates this fix, so nothing recorded why any sale fired. Reconstructing from price samples: 4 look like a market that had already vanished, 30 cannot be determined, and none look like a late trigger. That part is the easy fix — every future sale now writes its own trigger down at the moment it happens, so the question answers itself next rehearsal.

Did anything look different at quoting time?Q3 · spotting them earlier
No signal found

Size, price, mid and margin are near-identical between the sales and the healthy merges. Queue position was larger on the losers, but by a few hundred shares on a sample of 37 — that is noise, not a signal. The fields that would actually help (time to close, round-trip delay, opposite-side depth, spread, volume) were never recorded at all.

The one that wasn't a decision

The single largest rescue loss in the whole rehearsal was not a misjudged sale. It was a piece nobody sold at all.

filled  →  still alone after 2 h 54 m  →  ended only when the market settled
The rescue sweep gives up at 15 minutes. This piece sat alone for 2 hours 54 minutes (6 334 s, 9 053 s and 10 452 s on the three occasions) and only ended when the market itself settled.

All three of those held pieces are in the ladder above. The worst of them lost $1.54 — more than the worst actual sale. Selling everything on purpose is a rule change, so it did not ship here. It is written up as its own problem with its own rehearsal, and nothing in the live rules was touched.

Nothing was changed in the running system. This is a detective tool and a label. It opens past records strictly read-only — a test proves the data files come out byte-for-byte identical — and the new "why" label is only written after a sale has actually succeeded. The failure-safe order of decisions, the waiting periods, the price limits and every amount cap are exactly as they were.

How to see it yourself

The report reads a real rehearsal file. From the project folder:

python scripts/rescue_exit_report.py --registry data/01_shadow_12-09_00-58.db --top 4

You should see the losses worst-first with a plain-language trigger label on each, and the three held pieces flagged as AGED OUT. The written-up version of the same findings lives at docs/runs/2026-09-29-shadow01-rescue-exits.md.
